Conceptual coherence
Matías Osta-Vélez1, *
Peter Gärdenfors2

Abstract
Within theories of concepts and categorization, a concept is considered coherent to the extent that it is perceived as a structured whole. Although this notion
plays an important role in the study of conceptual processes such as categorization and reasoning, it has proven difficult to define and operationalize. In this
paper, we propose a formal framework in which the coherence of a concept
depends on the patterns of covariation among its properties. Combining the
theory of conceptual spaces with ideas from Principal Component Analysis,
we argue that the coherence of a concept can be estimated by how efficiently
its features can be compressed into a simpler, lower-dimensional structure. We
discuss how this view explains why natural kind concepts are generally seen
as more coherent than artifact concepts, and why coherent concepts support
stronger inductive inferences. Furthermore, we apply these ideas to the conjunction fallacy and propose that the drive for coherent representations can
override probabilistic intuitions when reasoning about social categories.
Keywords: Coherence • Concepts • Conceptual spaces • Principal component
analysis

Introduction

The main task of our cognitive system is to organize the constant and enormously diverse flow of stimuli it is exposed to with limited computational
and memory resources. It does this, in large part, by forming concepts that
allow it to group diverse inputs into equivalence classes, thus transforming
the overwhelming complexity of sensory data into manageable and useful
units of knowledge. Concepts, in this sense, can be seen as cognitive structures that compress and simplify information by identifying and retaining
the more relevant features of stimuli and discarding the less relevant ones.
Although all concepts simplify information, not all do so with the
same efficiency. For instance, the concept amethyst can be represented
compactly because its instances have diagnostic features (purple hue and
hexagonal prismatic shape) that make them immediately distinguishable
in almost any context.1 Its superordinate, quartz, includes a much wider
variety of instances, yet remains efficiently compressible: all members share
the same relatively small set of salient dimensions, such as crystal structure and chemical composition, which are, despite their internal diversity,
enough to distinguish them from other mineral categories. By contrast,
categories with less rigid structure and weaker regularities are harder to
compress, since greater exemplar diversity requires retaining more information. Consider, for example, the concept game, whose instances overlap
on only a handful of dimensions, such as rules, number of participants, or
scoring system, yet differ across many others, making them resistant to
compact representation (Wittgenstein, 2009). Something similar occurs
with very broad concepts, such as living being. The diversity of objects
encompassed by this category and the wide range of possible dimensions
that must be considered make the concept powerful in referential scope but
limited in its ability to convey precise information. This, in turn, renders it
less compressible and, as we will show later, less useful for reasoning and
prediction.2

In this paper, we argue that differences in how easily concepts can be
compressed correspond to differences in their perceived unity or coherence.
In other words, we propose that the conceptual system operates by reducing
informational complexity by forming representations that are as compact
as possible. The success of this process determines how coherent a concept
appears when it is employed in language or reasoning.
Discussing the coherence of concepts might not seem intuitive at first
glance, since the vast majority of our lexicalized everyday concepts already
have a certain degree of coherence. Indeed, the very fact that these concepts have stabilized in natural language (rather than being abandoned)
signals some degree of inferential and predictive utility (Goodman, 1983).
However, we can approach an intuitive or pre-theoretical understanding of
this notion by comparing an ad hoc concept, such as things to save from
a fire, with a natural kind concept, such as dog. The former, although not
arbitrary, has only minimal structure since the items it groups together are
highly diverse and the grouping itself may respond to idiosyncratic or personal criteria. The latter, by contrast, is a more robust and “non-accidental”
concept; it has a prototypical structure, its members are not merely collected for some temporary purpose, and they share many features, making
the concept inductively and informationally rich. Ultimately, the fact that
we perceive some concepts in natural language as more robust and less
arbitrary than others can be taken as evidence of our underlying intuitions
about coherence.3
Empirical research shows that coherence is an important factor in conceptual processes, especially in categorization and reasoning. For instance,
people tend to rely on basic-level concepts such as dog or chair rather than
broader ones like mammal or furniture when reasoning or categorizing,
since they are more coherent due to how well they balance generality and
informativeness (see Corter and Gluck, 1992). Another example comes from
mensionality” (Bellman, 1961): as the number of relevant dimensions increases, the
amount of information required to represent and generalize grows exponentially.

Psychologists study conceptual coherence using a variety of experimental tasks.
In some cases, they elicit relatively direct judgments about coherence by asking
participants how “uniform,” “informative,” or “natural” a particular category is
(Haslam et al., 2000). In other cases, they use indirect tasks such as property
induction, feature listing, and explanation generation. Rehder and Hastie (2004),
for example, use a property-induction task in which participants first learn a novel
category, such as “Lake Victoria Shrimp,” together with causal relations among
its features. They are then told that one exemplar has a new blank property, such
as “having slightly acidic mucus,” and are asked to estimate what proportion of
category members are likely to share that property. The results show that the
property is projected more when the exemplar respects the causal structure of the
category than when it does not. According to their view, coherence is a function of
causal structure. In feature-listing tasks, participants are asked to name as many
“essential” features of a category as they can, assuming that coherent categories
support richer descriptions (e.g., Patalano et al., 2006). In explanation-generation
tasks, participants are asked to explain why a member of a category has a particular
property or preference. The idea here is that members of coherent categories invite
deeper explanations than members of less coherent categories (e.g., Patalano et al.,
2003).

The nature of conceptual coherence

2.1

Previous models of coherence

In cognitive psychology, there are two main ways to explain conceptual
coherence. The first builds on the notion of similarity and suggests that
a concept is perceived as coherent when its instances share many features, forming a ‘natural grouping.’ In other words, greater within-category
similarity induces stronger perceived coherence. Rosch and Mervis (1975)
illustrate this view in their explanation of why basic-level concepts are
considered the most ‘cognitively natural’ and are consistently preferred in
tasks such as induction and categorization. The exemplars of these basiclevel concepts are very similar to each other and clearly different from
members of other groups. This creates a good balance between generality,
which is the range of examples included, and informativeness, which is how
much the members share properties that help us make useful inferences.
This balance is supposed to promote efficient reasoning.
However, this approach has limitations (Lassaline and Murphy, 1996).
In particular, similarity understood as a flat sum of shared properties fails
to capture the fact that certain dimensions of a concept are more important
than others and, consequently, some properties will carry more weight
than others when making similarity judgments. For example, within the
concept mammal, dog may be judged more similar to wolf than to cat if
the dimension physical appearance is emphasized, but this will be reversed
if a greater weight is assigned to the dimension domestication. In general,
any theory of coherence based on similarity should include a mechanism
for estimating the relative weights of a concept’s dimensions, and the
aforementioned view fails in this regard.6 Furthermore, and perhaps more

In what follows, we introduce the basic ideas behind conceptual spaces.
This theory will allow us to build a more precise and comprehensive account
of conceptual coherence; one that overcomes the limitations identified in
Thagard’s approach.

2.2

A new proposal: Conceptual spaces,
heterogeneity, and coherence

2.2.1 Conceptual spaces
As we said in the introduction, the core of our proposal is that conceptual
coherence is related to the ‘covariational structure’ of concepts, that is, the
extent to which their properties systematically co-occur across exemplars.
A further central claim of this article is that, among existing theories of
concepts, the theory of conceptual spaces (Gärdenfors, 2000, 2014) provides
the most suitable framework for developing this account.
According to this theory, concepts are represented as regions in a multidimensional space, with each axis corresponding to a ‘quality dimension’
that encodes a specific aspect of the concept. For example, in the concept
fruit, relevant dimensions might include color, size, taste and texture. Some
dimensions can be specified independently of others, as in the case of color

6

and size in this example, while others can only be meaningfully specified
together, such as hue and its intensity when we try to represent colors. The
former are called ‘separable’ dimensions, and the latter ‘integral’ dimensions (cf. M. Jones and Goldstone, 2013). Any set of integral dimensions
that is separable from any other dimension is called a domain.
The theory of conceptual spaces makes a distinction between ‘properties’ and ‘concepts.’ Properties, such as the color red, are convex regions within a single domain (in this case, the color domain) and are
considered the simplest form of conceptual representation. In contrast,
concepts cover several separate dimensions and domains and are represented as convex regions in the aggregate space of those domains. The
dimensions and domains that constitute a concept often vary together. For
example, in the concept fruit, dimensions such as size and weight, as well
as ripeness, color, and taste, usually vary together. These patterns generate
expectations that help us make inferences; in other words, knowing one
property of an exemplar can help predict others (Osta-Vélez and Gärdenfors,
2020, 2022).
Within this framework, an instance of a concept (i.e., a particular object)
is represented by a point in a multidimensional space, and the similarity
between two instances is a monotonically decreasing function of their distance in this space. It is common to assume that the metric of the space is
Euclidean, although other metrics may be used if the context requires it
(Shepard, 1964; Johannesson, 2002; Gärdenfors, 2014). Representing exemplars as points, properties as convex regions within single domains, and
concepts as convex regions spanning multiple domains provides a mathematically precise way to capture multiple conceptual processes within a single formal framework. Crucially, as we will see later, this formal framework
provides tools for analyzing statistical aspects of conceptual representation,
which will be fundamental to our analysis of coherence.

2.2.2 Heterogeneity and coherence
Now, how can this theory help us elucidate the notion of coherence? In the
introduction, we explained that the coherence of a concept refers to the

PhiMiSci | Vol. 7

degree to which the diversity of its exemplars is nonetheless perceived as
“unified”: the more diverse the members of a category, the harder it is to
achieve that unity. Psychologists call this overall dispersion of variation
the heterogeneity of a concept (S. A. Gelman, 2003; Brandone, 2017). Within
conceptual spaces, the heterogeneity of a concept corresponds to the volume
of the region representing the concept in the space: the larger the volume,
the greater the variability of its instances.
Heterogeneity is directly related to the uncertainty we face when
reasoning about new instances of a category. For instance, when we learn
that an object is a pipe wrench, the strong similarity among its instances
in terms of shape, function, and materials allows us to predict many of its
properties with confidence; whereas learning that an object is a handbag
leaves much greater uncertainty due to the wider variation in size, material,
shape, and purpose that this concept involves. In general, the narrower and
more specialized the concept, the more specific and reliable the inferences
it supports (cf. Thagard and Nisbett, 1982; Sloman and Lagnado, 2005;
Brandone, 2017). In conceptual spaces, this idea can be expressed more
or less straightforwardly. When we categorize an object, we place it as a
point within the region that represents a concept. The area of this region is
a function of the range of properties the object might possess (the larger
the space, the more possibilities exist). As we learn new properties of the
object or sort it into a more specific concept, we narrow this region, thus
shrinking the space of possibilities and reducing our uncertainty about its
unknown properties.
Yet, heterogeneity does not fully determine the inductive power of
concepts. A concept can occupy a large area of a conceptual space (i.e., it
encompasses a wide range of variation) and still be highly informative if
its properties are strongly interconnected. In other words, if the properties
covered by a heterogeneous concept covary, such that knowing one of
them allows me to infer others, then the uncertainty associated with that
concept can be reduced and property induction facilitated. This is the key
point of our analysis of coherence: it concerns not the amount of variation
a category exhibits (its heterogeneity) in isolation, but the way in which
that variation is organized. To formalize this, we propose measuring the

coherence of a concept by estimating the extent to which the total variance
of a concept can be explained in terms of a minimum number of covarying
dimensions, which will reflect the extent to which its properties “move
together” in conceptual space.
The covariational patterns we refer to are evident in animal categories. For example, an animal’s body size often covaries with attributes
such as lifespan, diet, and habitat; the fact that larger mammals generally
live longer than insects illustrates this. Similarly, the specific shape of a
bird’s beak is a cue to multiple correlated attributes like diet, habitat, nesting behavior, and even mating calls. Research by Younger (1990), Billman
and Knutson (1996), and Hayes et al. (1996) shows that humans excel at
detecting such covariations across many domains. It seems reasonable to
assume that this has an evolutionary explanation. Recognizing patterns in
the environment is crucial for the survival of individuals, aiding in predicting potential dangers and identifying food sources and possible shelters.
The result has been the development and refinement of an innate ability to
group sets of properties that covary. This idea is consistent with Rosch’s
(1975) prototype theory, which highlights basic-level concepts as important
clusters of features that often co-occur (Holland et al., 1986, pp. 183–4).
The question that emerges from the previous discussion is the following: which cognitive mechanisms represent these covariations and how
might they work? We speculate that the detection of correlated features
might function similarly to principal component analysis (PCA). In the
next section, we explore how PCA can systematically capture and quantify
these covariations.

Principal component analysis as a
measure of conceptual coherence

3.1

Principal component analysis and conceptual
coherence

PCA is a statistical method for data analysis that reduces the complexity
of a dataset while preserving as much of its structure as possible. Broadly
speaking, PCA takes a set of observations (or ‘data points’) represented
in an n-dimensional space and returns a set of m orthogonal dimensions
(with m ≤ n), also referred to as ‘principal components’, which account for
the maximum possible variance of the original data.
One way to visualize this process is to imagine a cluster of points
in a high-dimensional space, each representing an exemplar defined by
many features. What PCA does is analogous to ‘rotating’ this cluster to
find the axis along which the cloud of points spreads the most; this is the
direction that defines the first principal component and which captures the
largest portion of the total variation. Because the first principal component
captures the largest portion of overall variation, the proportion of variance
it explains indicates how strongly the features covary. In other words, the
more variance accounted for by this first principal component, the more
coherent (or systematically interrelated) the cluster of points is. Subsequent
principal components, orthogonal to the first, capture progressively smaller
portions of the remaining variance (see Figure 1).
PCA is widely used as a dimensionality reduction technique in areas
such as machine learning, pattern recognition, or data visualization. When
datasets have too many variables, PCA can reduce their dimensionality
while preserving the most crucial information. We find it plausible to
assume that this same principle is used in concept representation, which
typically includes a large number of exemplars represented along a large
number of dimensions. More specifically, we propose that the coherence of
a concept can be estimated by the proportion of total variance explained
by the first principal component(s). In a conceptual space, the instances
of a concept appear as points that may cluster more or less tightly; by

convey and understand complex ideas without getting distracted by details.
This approach is consistent with the principle of information economy,
which aims to communicate the greatest amount of information with the

concepts, different clusters of covarying features may emerge and, consequently, new principal components encoding them. In general, the more
variance that can be expressed in fewer principal components, the more

by compressing and simplifying the vast amount of information it deals

with is not new. Edelman and Intrator (1997) have argued that one of the
central functions of learning is to extract low-dimensional representations
from high-dimensional input spaces. According to them, perceptual and
conceptual learning alike can be understood as processes of dimensionality
reduction, in which the system identifies a small set of latent variables that
explain most of the variation in the data, thereby enabling both efficient
storage and generalization. Another example comes from the study of
memory, where the notion of dimensionality reduction is regularly used.
Kerrén et al. (2025), for instance, propose that episodic memory relies on
the compression of high-dimensional information about our experiences
into simpler representations that can be retrieved with relative ease. This
compression is supposed to reduce interference among stored memories
while preserving the relational structure needed for flexible retrieval.

3.3

Salience, covariance and a measure of
coherence

The use of formal methods in psychology often poses challenges because
the phenomena under study rarely fit neatly into the formal structures
intended to model them. An important challenge in using PCA to analyze
conceptual representation is that, in its standard form, all dimensions are
treated as equally relevant. But, as noted in Section 2.1, our representation
of concepts always involves weighted dimensions of varying psychological
relevance. For example, in the concept dog, attributes like size or coat
are typically more influential than features such as body temperature. To
address this problem, we now examine in more detail how PCA works and
propose a way of incorporating salience weighting to more realistically
reflect the internal structure of a concept.
The standard method for applying PCA is as follows (Jolliffe, 2002).
First, the (continuous) variables of the original dataset are standardized to
prevent specific dimensions from dominating the analysis due to differences
in scale. Second, a correlation matrix is computed to capture how pairs
of variables vary together, allowing for the identification of potential
correlations among dimensions. Finally, the eigenvectors and eigenvalues

10

Matías Osta-Vélez & Peter Gärdenfors

of this matrix are calculated in order to determine the principal components;
each eigenvector defines a direction of variance, while its corresponding
eigenvalue indicates the magnitude of that variance. The main components
are then ordered by their related eigenvalues, with the first main component
explaining the most variation in the dataset.
The problem lies in the first step, since standardization treats all dimensions as equally relevant. To avoid this, we propose weighting the data for
each dimension (i.e., multiplying it by a weight vector) before calculating
the covariance matrix, so that the model reflects the relative psychological
salience of each attribute. This adjustment allows psychologically salient
dimensions to ‘guide’ the analysis, rather than letting dimensions with high
numeric variance but low importance take over the calculation.
It is important to note that covariance measures require a reference
point because they are based on deviations from that point. Standard PCA
implicitly uses the mean as this reference when standardizing data. However, since we reject standardization precisely because dimensions differ in
psychological importance, we must explicitly choose a ‘cognitively meaningful reference.’ To do this, we introduce the idea of a universal prototype
(representing the prototype of ‘thing’) which we represent by the mean
of the positions of all objects. This universal prototype serves as a more
nuanced reference for understanding how different features relate within
a concept. We take the universal prototype to serve as the origin of the
conceptual space. The role of the universal prototype becomes particularly
clear when considering concepts that exhibit little or no variation in most
of their dimensions. Even when individual dimensions do not correlate
strongly, covariance remains meaningful because it is assessed relative to
the entire conceptual space. Many animal concepts, for example tiger,
illustrate this point. For such categories, there will be little or no direct
correlation between their dimensions, yet their overall covariance structure
remains stable when measured against the universal prototype. This is
the reason why we use covariance rather than correlation to determine the
coherence of a concept.

3.4

Some properties of this coherence measure

We can now examine the five key features that shape our coherence measure.
First, the measure is dependent on the salience values of the domains. If a
domain has low salience, then all volume related to it will be small, and,
consequently, its contribution to the total coherence of the concept will be
small. For example, consider reclassification of the concept whale from
fish to mammal. In earlier taxonomical systems, the ecological domain
(living in water) was more salient than key ‘biological’ properties such as
warm-bloodedness, live birth, and milk production (Gärdenfors, 2000, Section
6.4). However, by shifting salience toward these biological dimensions and
away from purely ecological ones, whale became better aligned with the

Conceptual coherence

core properties of mammal. This shift improved the internal consistency of
the concept, thereby improving its overall conceptual coherence.
Second, this measure is statistical, not probabilistic. It is defined for any
distribution of points in the space, independently of probabilities. We do
not assume that cognitive agents calculate probabilities when estimating
conceptual coherence. More broadly, our proposal could be understood as
statistical or data-driven since it builds on the idea that agents extract relational patterns from their experience representing instances in a conceptual
space.
Third, concepts that contain many salient dimensions that covary will
be seen as more coherent than those that contain fewer such dimensions.
This helps explain why natural categories are coherent, as they usually
contain several salient dimensions that covary. In contrast, artifact categories have few salient dimensions (in many cases, only function is salient)
and do not necessarily covary with other dimensions. Consequently, their
dimensions exhibit weaker covariance, leading to lower coherence.
Fourth, even among concepts with multiple salient dimensions, coherence increases further when these dimensions form tightly interrelated
clusters of properties. This dense clustering is common in natural concepts
and it helps to explain why they are easier to learn than artifact concepts
(Billman and Knutson, 1996; Kornblith, 1993; Shipley, 1993).
Fifth, it follows from our definition that superordinate concepts are
less coherent than their subordinates; even though their broader scope
can exhibit higher pairwise covariation, the large volume disperses variance across many principal components, so the first few capture a smaller
proportion of the total.
It might be objected that, since distances in conceptual spaces represent
similarity, our proposal is subject to the criticism against similarity account
of coherence that we presented in section 2.1. However, we avoid the
arbitrariness of similarity by assuming that weights are assigned to the
dimensions before coherence is measured. A consequence of this approach
is that there is a certain context dependence of the coherence of a concept
depending on the assignment of weights. Our example of the change in the
concept whale illustrates this.

We have shown that PCA, when modified to incorporate dimensional
salience and a universal reference point, offers a method for estimating
conceptual coherence. This framework helps explain key cognitive phenomena, such as why natural kind concepts tend to be more coherent than
artifacts and why concepts with tightly clustered properties are easier to
learn. In the following section, we explore how coherence relates to causal
knowledge and its role in inductive reasoning.

4

Coherence, causality, and induction

4.1

Coherence and causality

Some researchers have argued that coherence cannot be fully explained
by covariational learning alone and suggest that causal knowledge plays
a central role instead. In a series of experiments, Malt and Smith (1984)
and Ahn et al. (2002) found that typicality judgments (i.e., assessments
of how representative an exemplar is of a given concept) were stronger
when the exemplar contained pairs of causally related properties. Focusing
on the concept bird, they presented to subjects descriptions of exemplars
that shared general features like having wings but differed in other specific
properties: some exemplars had the properties lives near the ocean and
eats fish, while others had is white and eats fish. Although both property
pairs exhibited similar covariation, participants rated exemplars with lives
near the ocean and eats fish as more typical, perceiving these properties as
causally linked. The researchers concluded that semantic judgments depend
not only on pairwise covariation but also on causal relationships, a finding
consistent with ideas from the theory-theory, particularly its interpretation
of conceptual coherence.
There is, however, an alternative explanation of these results (see also
Rogers and McClelland, 2004). It is possible that subjects perceive two
features as causally associated, and weight them more heavily in their
typicality judgments, because both belong to larger clusters of correlated

12

Matías Osta-Vélez & Peter Gärdenfors

properties that are present in many exemplars of a concept. In most shorebirds, for instance, lives near the ocean and eats fish co-occur with properties
such as can swim, has long legs, and has a long neck. By contrast, properties
like is white and eats fish are more dispersed within bird, since they appear
in highly dissimilar birds; the white tern, for example, is white and eats
fish yet has no physical resemblance to most shorebirds. As a consequence,
the perceived coherence between two properties could stem from their
covariation with the other properties of the concept, which is what the first
principal components capture. The asymmetry observed in these experiments therefore follows from the measure we propose, without any need
to posit causal knowledge.
Our intuitions about the relationship between two properties within
a concept may also be influenced by our knowledge of how those same
properties relate in other concepts within the same semantic domain. For
instance, people might see the properties is white and eats fish as uncorrelated because they recognize that color and dietary habits do not systematically covary in most animal concepts. In contrast, the pair lives near
the ocean and eats fish may appear correlated because habitat and dietary
habits typically covary through multiple animal concepts. Overall, this
suggests that conceptual coherence may be influenced by the statistical
structure of the entire semantic domain (that is, groups of concepts) rather
than by that of a single concept (see Billman and Dávila, 2001).
Following a similar line of argument that sees causality as the key factor
in coherence, Rehder (2017) and Rehder and Kim (2006, 2010) examined
the ‘coherence effect’ in judgments of concept instances. Their findings
suggest that people are more likely to judge an exemplar as a good category
member if it preserves the learned causal relationships between its features,
what Rehder and Hastie (2001) refer to as the causal laws of the concept.
As an example, consider the concept tropical frog: Suppose people
have learned that being poisonous (feature A) causes brightly coloured
skin (feature B) in these frogs. Given this causal relationship, people judge
exemplars of frogs that are not poisonous and not brightly colored (¬A¬B)
as better category members than exemplars that are either not poisonous
but brightly coloured or poisonous but not brightly coloured (¬AB or A¬B).

PhiMiSci | Vol. 7

This preference arises because ¬A¬B exemplars preserve the expected
causal structure (“no poison, therefore no bright color”), whereas ¬AB
and A¬B violate it, as their features do not align with the learned causal
relationship.
Our approach explains this effect by looking at the loadings and scores
in the relevant PCA‑reduced conceptual space. In PCA, each principal
component is defined by a set of loadings, i.e., weights taken from the
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, that quantify how strongly each
original dimension contributes to that axis. When two dimensions share
large, same-signed loadings, they co-vary, and the component points along
their joint direction. The scores are the coordinates of the exemplars when
projected onto the new axes; they show how well an exemplar aligns with
that covariation pattern.
When subjects learn the causal rule “poison causes brightness,” the
dimensions encoding these two properties become strongly positively correlated in the (hypothetical) sample of frogs that learners generate from
the rule. PCA therefore places this relation on its first component, with
both dimensions receiving large, same-signed loadings. Exemplars that
are both poisonous and bright (AB) are projected far along the positive
side of the first PC, while those that are neither (¬A¬B) are projected far
along the negative side. In contrast, mismatched cases (A¬B or ¬AB) have
contributions that cancel out on this axis and instead receive large scores
on an orthogonal “mismatch” component. Coherence can then be described
in two equivalent ways: rule-consistent exemplars lie close to the first
principal component and have large absolute scores on it, something that
indicates a strong alignment with the main causal pattern of the concept. In
contrast, mismatched exemplars lie farther from this axis and have scores
closer to zero, indicating a weaker alignment with these patterns. The
judged typicality of exemplars decreases as this alignment weakens, which
is why AB and ¬A¬B are considered more coherent than A¬B or ¬AB.
To be clear, we do not claim that causality is irrelevant to conceptual
coherence. However, we believe that outside of expert knowledge, most
causal understanding of inter-feature relations is likely shallow; for example,
while most people recognize that the curved shape of a boomerang or the

Conceptual coherence

13

size of a bird’s wings plays a causal role in (or are enabling conditions of)
their respective flight patterns, few individuals really grasp the underlying
physical mechanisms that govern these causal relationships.
We believe that much everyday causal knowledge is acquired through
causal generics, that is, statements like “birds fly because they have wings.”
Although these kinds of statements only highlight causal relationships
without accounting for the underlying mechanisms, they provide two types
of valuable information: (1) they signal a positive pattern of co-occurrence
between two dimensions of a concept (wings and flight tend to coexist),
and (2) they establish an expectation of asymmetric intervention (removing
wings eliminates flight, but inhibiting flight does not affect the presence of
wings). In this way, they act as cognitive shortcuts which allow subjects to
internalize both the correlation and its causal direction without needing to
infer them from individual experience through repeated examples.
We speculate that many folk concepts are not formed by a purely datadriven process but are structured around causal generics that establish
the covariational structure, which is later refined by further learning. Still,
coherence is more dependent on detecting covariation between properties
than on an explicit understanding of causal relationships.
In summary, while causal knowledge contributes to the perceived
coherence of concepts, its influence is mostly limited to expert knowledge.
In everyday reasoning (particularly for folk concepts), coherence seems to
be guided by statistical mechanisms that identify patterns of covariation
and plausible enabling conditions rather than by a deep understanding of
causal relationships.

4.2

Coherence and induction

Empirical studies consistently show a positive correlation between coherence and the confidence with which concepts are used in inductive tasks. In
an influential study, S. A. Gelman (1988) found that children exhibit greater
sensitivity to conceptual coherence, since they tend to make more inductive
inferences about natural kinds than about artifact concepts. For example,
tiger supports a wide range of inductive inferences, while clock does not,

PhiMiSci | Vol. 7

since different kinds of clocks share few properties beyond keeping time.
The latter exhibits weaker correlations among its properties, meaning its
principal component would account for less variance.
Rehder and Hastie (2004) further showed that inductive generalizations
tend to be more robust when they involve coherent concepts. Specifically,
they showed that the typicality effect observed in category-based induction
(see Osta-Vélez and Gärdenfors, 2020) can be interpreted as a coherence
effect in the following sense: atypical members of a concept support weaker
generalizations than more typical members because they violate the expected correlations associated with that concept.
Our approach offers a rather straightforward explanation of the role
of coherence in inductive inferences. Consider within-category induction,
where we predict new properties of a categorical input from some already
known property (for example, “x is a red apple; thus, x is sweet”). This
form of induction clearly relies on our knowledge of correlations. The
more coherent our categories are, the better they enable us to make such
inductive inferences, helping us manage the uncertainty inherent in basic
conceptual information (cf. Osta-Vélez and Gärdenfors, 2022).
Coherence also plays a role in between-categories induction. For example,
in category-based induction, people rely on the overall perceived similarity
between two concepts to project a property of one to the other (Douven
et al., 2023). However, induction is not always driven by overall similarities;
sometimes our knowledge about covariation between properties takes
precedence. An example of this can be found in the work of Heit and
Rubinstein (1994), who showed that people sometimes reason relying on
relationships between the properties of concepts rather than their overall
similarity. For example, they found that in inductive tasks involving animal
behavior, people judged tiger and hawk to be more similar when focusing
on specific properties like nocturnal feeding habits than the pair chicken
and hawk, even though the latter pair has higher overall similarity.

about social concepts and we offer a coherence-based analysis of the conjunction fallacy.

Coherence, social concepts, and the
conjunction fallacy

So far, we have sought to explain why our cognitive system strives to
construct and prioritize coherent concepts, using natural kind concepts as
the primary example. However, we also argue that social concepts, such as
banker, guitarist, or feminist, are especially influenced by coherence and,
consequently, by correlations (Patalano et al., 2006). Experimental evidence
supports this claim. For instance, Nguyen and Chevalier (2015) discovered
that 5-year-olds favor coherent concepts when making inductive decisions
about social concepts. In one of their experiments, participants were told
the following: “Baseball players like apples, and board game players like
bananas. This is Pat. Pat is both a baseball player (coherent concept) and
a board game player (incoherent concept).” When asked whether they
believed Pat would prefer an apple or a banana, most chose the fruit linked
to the coherent concept.
Social and natural kind concepts diverge significantly in how they
support categorization. Natural concepts typically include many observable
and behavioral features and tend to be stable across cultural and social contexts. In contrast, social concepts involve many different kinds of features,
ranging from observable elements to behavioral, epistemic, or ideological
dispositions; furthermore, many of them are socially constructed and they
tend to be less stable over time and across cultures. More importantly,
the two kinds of concepts have different informational profiles. Natural
concepts typically provide precise expectations about the distribution of
properties among their exemplars and resist cross-classification at the same
hierarchical level. Social concepts, by contrast, allow for a high degree of
cross-classification: individuals often belong to multiple social categories
simultaneously. Even when stereotypes are used to manage the variability
encountered in social categorization, no single social concept exhaustively
determines the other traits a person may have. For instance, someone categorized as a scientist can also be categorized as a musician, a parent, a
liberal, or an introvert as social roles and personal traits overlap freely.
This suggests that when we learn about and reason about people,

PhiMiSci | Vol. 7

our process resembles ‘inference learning’ (E. L. Jones and Ross, 2011;
Yamauchi et al., 2002).9 That is, we attempt to predict which additional
social categories an individual might belong to by drawing on our general
knowledge of correlations and selecting those that best align with our prior
understanding of the person. In essence, if we have certain prior knowledge
about an individual x, we expect x to exhibit properties that are positively
correlated with this knowledge and to lack properties that are negatively
correlated with it.
This idea offers a straightforward explanation of the conjunction fallacy
from a coherentist perspective. In their well-known experiment, Tversky
and Kahneman (1983) presented participants with a description of Linda, a
31-year-old woman who was single, outspoken, highly intelligent, and a
philosophy major. As a student, she was deeply engaged in issues of discrimination and social justice and participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations.
Participants were then asked to assess the likelihood of two scenarios:
1. Linda being a bank teller
2. Linda being both a bank teller and a feminist.
Despite the probability law stating that the likelihood of two events occurring together is always less than or equal to the probability of either event
occurring alone, most participants chose the latter scenario, demonstrating
a ‘conjunction fallacy.’
From our perspective, this fallacy happens because the property of
being a feminist is strongly correlated with the set of attributes previously
described about Linda. The scenario of Linda as a feminist bank teller
increases the coherence of our concept of Linda compared to merely being
a bank teller. In other words, if our cognitive system favors conceptual
coherence, it is more reasonable to predict that Linda is a feminist bank
teller, as this classification is consistent with the provided information.
By contrast, categorizing her as only a bank teller introduces uncertainty
about additional properties she might have, particularly those positively

6

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a formal framework for analyzing conceptual
coherence and a method for quantifying it. We argued that coherence reflects the compressibility of the covariational structure of concepts (i.e., the
clusters of properties that systematically co-occur across category exemplars) such that more coherent concepts can be compressed into simpler
dimensional structures.
Our proposal contributes to the discussion of concepts in several ways.
First, it clarifies the relationship between coherence and inductive reasoning. Second, it bridges rival theories of conceptual structure by showing
how a similarity-centered view can account (at least partially) for the role
of causal knowledge in the coherence of concepts. In addition, as we explain in Section 4.1, our approach can account for theory-like phenomena
without committing to the existence of innate intuitive theories and, in this
sense, we believe it has an advantage over the theory-theory view when it
comes to explaining coherence. Third, it offers a computationally tractable
model that could be applied in cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and
semantic modeling.
This model also accounts for different empirical findings in categorization and reasoning. It explains why natural concepts exhibit stronger
coherence than artifacts; why concepts with tightly interrelated features
are easier to learn; and why social concepts influence probabilistic reasoning, particularly in cases such as the conjunction fallacy. Our model
provides a principled explanation for these phenomena by linking coherence to underlying statistical structures rather than treating it as a primitive
notion.
Additionally, our theory yields a series of predictions and opens some
new lines of inquiry. One prediction is that a non-convex concept with

16

Matías Osta-Vélez & Peter Gärdenfors

instances drawn from disjoint regions of a conceptual space will be incoherent because the first principal component would capture the between-group
separation rather than within-group covariance and, as a consequence, no
single principal component would dominate the analysis of variance.
A second prediction, mentioned in Section 4.1, is that exemplars of
a concept judged as ‘typical’ should obtain high scores in the PCA space
because they tend to align with the concept’s most significant covariation
patterns. Conversely, atypical exemplars should obtain lower scores as they
fail to align with these patterns. In other words, our model provides tools
to explain typicality effects in terms of data compressibility.
A third prediction is that if a new dimension (domain) that correlates
with the first principal component(s) is added to the representation of a
concept, then the overall coherence of the concept is strengthened. An
example of this is the emergence of the notion of “gene” and its impact on
the coherence of the concept living thing.
Future research could focus on the relationship between coherence and
psychological essentialism, that is, the tendency to believe that categories
possess hidden essences. The experimental literature on essentialism provides robust evidence that our essentialist intuitions are associated with the
identification of clusters of co-varying properties (S. A. Gelman, 2003). If
we assume that a process akin to PCA underlies our conceptual processing,
then it would be reasonable to hypothesize that essences correspond to
the principal component(s) of a concept (Gärdenfors and Osta-Vélez, 2026).
Further investigations of this idea could provide deeper insights into how
people perceive conceptual structure and form inductive generalizations.
Finally, we should note that there are methods similar to PCA that may
be equally or even better suited for quantifying coherence. For instance,
Mixed-Variable Factor Analysis or Multiple Correspondence Analysis could
be useful for analyzing concepts that involve both continuous and discrete dimensions (Chavent et al., 2022). Future work could focus on these
methodological aspects, which, due to space constraints, have been relatively sidelined in the present paper.
